# Thread — "The open ground-floor shop under a 1970s block is a red flag, not a feature"

**1/**
A 1970s concrete-frame building with an open-plan ground-floor shop looks like nothing to most buyers. To a structural inspector, it's the classic signature of a soft-story mechanism — one of the most consistent predictors of earthquake collapse worldwide.

**2/**
Here's the regulatory gap: Portugal's DL 53/2018 mandates seismic-safety assessment (RAVS) only for buildings over 28m, hospitals, schools, and large residential. Thousands of vulnerable 1970s–80s *prédios* fall under that threshold. Nobody is legally required to ever check them.

**3/**
A building can fail Eurocode 8 "Life Safety" checks outright and remain legally occupied — because nothing in the current framework triggers a mandatory retrofit. Legal occupancy and structural safety are not the same claim.

**4/**
The paradox: heavy rigid masonry infill walls are acoustically excellent — they're what kills noise between units. But without seismic gaps (*juntas sísmicas*), that same rigid infill concentrates mass and stiffness, producing short-column shear failure. The fix for noise and the fix for earthquakes pull in opposite directions.

**5/**
Ground-floor retail conversions often illegally remove infill walls to open up the space — creating a soft-story collapse mechanism while simultaneously destroying the acoustic isolation above. One bad renovation decision, two separate failure modes.

**6/**
Field red flags anyone can check before calling an engineer: diagonal/X shear cracks in columns or walls, exposed corroding rebar at ground-floor columns, infill walls with no visible corner ties to the frame. Any of these means stop and escalate — this is Tier 1 triage territory.

**7/**
The international benchmark that makes this concrete: after the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake, pre-1981 Japanese buildings overwhelmingly failed while post-1981 code-compliant buildings overwhelmingly survived. Pre-code construction isn't a theoretical risk category — it's an empirically measured one.
