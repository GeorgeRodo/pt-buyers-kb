## 1. What ISO/IEC 17020:2026 is

ISO/IEC 17020:2026 (*Conformity Assessment — Requirements for Bodies Performing Inspections*) was published 27 March 2026, superseding the 2012 edition. It is the international standard that defines what an inspection body must have, do, and prove to produce inspection results that can be relied upon by third parties — regulators, courts, financial institutions, and insurers.

The standard is published by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC). It is part of the ISO 17000 series on conformity assessment, which also includes ISO/IEC 17025 (testing and calibration laboratories) and ISO/IEC 17065 (product certification bodies). These are the frameworks that underpin the safety and reliability infrastructure of global markets.

An accredited inspection body under ISO/IEC 17020:2026 has been independently verified — by a national accreditation body — to meet every requirement the standard sets. In Portugal, that body is **IPAC (Instituto Português de Acreditação)**, a full signatory to the European Accreditation (EA) Multilateral Agreement and the ILAC (International Laboratory Accreditation Cooperation) Mutual Recognition Arrangement. IPAC accreditation means the inspection results are accepted across all EA and ILAC signatories — the entire EU and most of the developed world.

**The 2026 revision matters strategically.** Legacy inspection bodies operating under the 2012 version face a mandatory transition to the 2026 standard, with a deadline of approximately March 2029. InspectOS builds its Quality Management System natively to ISO/IEC 17020:2026 from day one — bypassing all transition friction and achieving compliant status before competitors who started earlier under 2012.

## 2. The three inspection body types — and why only Type A works

ISO/IEC 17020:2026 defines three categories of inspection body based on their relationship to the parties involved in what they inspect:

| Type | Definition | Independence level | Suitable for PCA? |
|------|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Type A** | Third-party body fully independent of all parties involved in design, manufacture, supply, installation, ownership, use, or maintenance of inspected items | Highest | **Yes — required** |
| Type B | Separate and identifiable part of an organisation, providing inspection services only to its parent | Medium | No |
| Type C | Part of an organisation that may also be involved in the items it inspects | Lower | No |

InspectOS operates as a **Type A inspection body**. This is not a choice — it is a requirement. Here is why:

**Property condition assessments have legal, financial, and regulatory consequences.** When a PCA report is used to support a mortgage application, an insurance underwriting decision, a regulatory compliance declaration, or a civil court proceeding, the independence of the inspection body is directly relevant to the weight of the report. A Type B or Type C body — one with organisational ties to developers, property managers, or financial institutions with interests in the outcome — cannot provide the demonstrable independence that these applications require.

**IMPIC cannot mandate a Type B or Type C report.** If InspectOS's inspection body had any financial or operational link to a developer, a bank, or a property manager, IMPIC would be mandating a document produced by a party with a commercial interest in the outcome. That is legally and politically untenable. The Type A classification — which requires proof of independence via legal entity structure, financial independence, and management autonomy — is the only configuration that enables regulatory mandate.

**Courts require it.** In civil litigation over property defects, both parties produce expert opinions. A Type A accredited body's report has a structurally different evidentiary status than an unaccredited engineer's opinion — the court can examine the accreditation scope, the documented methodology, and the QA process. An informal inspection has no comparable audit trail.

## 3. What ISO/IEC 17020:2026 actually requires

The standard is structured around eight core chapters. Each creates requirements that informal or self-declared inspectors cannot meet — not because they lack skill, but because the standard requires institutional processes, documentation, and oversight that individual practitioners cannot maintain alone.

### 3.1 Impartiality and independence (Clause 4)

The inspection body must systematically identify, assess, and mitigate all risks to impartiality on an ongoing basis. This includes:

- Documented policies ensuring personnel are free from commercial, financial, or other pressure that could compromise judgement
- An impartiality risk register, reviewed at least annually
- Management commitment documented at the top level
- No ownership or control links to parties with interests in inspection outcomes

**What this means in practice:** Before every inspection, InspectOS must document that no conflict of interest exists between the inspector and the property, the owner, the agent, or any party in the transaction. This is not a declaration — it is a managed process with an audit trail.

### 3.2 Structural requirements (Clause 5)

The body must have a defined legal identity, an organisational chart with clearly documented responsibilities, and management oversight of the entire inspection function. It cannot be an informal partnership or an individual trading as a company.

**Implication for legal entity structure:** InspectOS's inspection body should be established as a legally distinct entity from the PCS analytics platform. IPAC assessors will examine whether operating the scoring database creates a financial interest in inspection volume — a potential impartiality risk under Clause 4.1. The cleanest structure separates the inspection body (e.g., *InspectOS Avaliações, Lda.*) from the data platform. Both can be FAIRBANK Group subsidiaries; they must have distinct governance.

### 3.3 Personnel competence (Clause 6)

Every inspector must have documented qualifications, a defined training programme, formal authorisation to conduct inspections, continuous monitoring of performance, and ongoing continuing education. The body must demonstrate that its competency framework identifies what knowledge and skills each inspection type requires and how each inspector has met those requirements.

**The PCA inspector certification framework** (PCA-I Trainee → PCA-II Authorised → PCA-III Senior) is designed to meet Clause 6.1 exactly. The 40-hour foundation course, 50 supervised inspections, 80-hour advanced course, and annual continuing education requirements map directly to the standard's personnel competence demands.

### 3.4 Facilities and equipment (Clause 6.2)

Equipment used in inspections must be identified, maintained, and calibrated. Calibration records must be retrievable and traceable. Moisture meters, thermal imaging cameras, electrical testers, and other instruments must have documented calibration histories.

**This is frequently overlooked.** Many informal inspectors use equipment with no calibration records. An accredited body cannot use equipment without a documented calibration history — any inspection result produced with uncalibrated equipment is non-compliant and must be flagged.

### 3.5 Inspection methods (Clause 7.1)

Inspection procedures must be documented. The body cannot rely on individual inspectors applying informal knowledge — it must have written procedures specifying what is inspected, how it is inspected, what evidence is recorded, and what constitutes conformity or non-conformity for each building system.

The PCA methodology — six dimensions (Structural, MEP Systems, Building Envelope, Life Safety, Deferred Maintenance, Systems Obsolescence) with documented inspection protocols, measurement criteria, and severity grades — is the artefact that satisfies Clause 7.1.

### 3.6 Inspection records (Clause 7.3)

Records must be retrievable, traceable to the inspector who produced them, and retained for a minimum period. The record must enable the inspection to be repeated under the same conditions — meaning every photograph, observation, and measurement is documented with date, location, equipment ID, and inspector ID.

**The 10-year retention policy** in the PCS database satisfies this requirement and creates the historical data depth that makes PCS analytics possible.

### 3.7 Inspection reports (Clause 7.4)

Every report must include: identification of the issuing body, unique report ID and issue date, inspection dates, property identification (linked to NPV), authorised inspector signature, and results with conformity statements. Optional elements that strengthen legal weight: methodology referenced, measurement uncertainty where applicable, deviations from agreed scope.

The machine-readable JSON output of the PCR (Property Condition Report), combined with the human-readable PDF, satisfies Clause 7.4 and enables seamless API ingestion for B2B clients.

### 3.8 Complaints and appeals (Clauses 7.5–7.6)

The body must have a documented process for receiving, investigating, and resolving complaints — with independent review, tracking, and formal closure. This is the **largest gap in current Portuguese inspection practice** (estimated 85% gap against the standard). It is also one of the easiest to close with proper documentation design.

### 3.9 Management system (Clause 8)

Under Option A (recommended for a purpose-built inspection body), a dedicated QMS covering quality policy, document control, record control, management review, internal audits, corrective actions, and preventive actions. Essentially: ISO 9001 adapted specifically to meet inspection body requirements.

## 5. The IPAC accreditation pathway

IPAC (Instituto Português de Acreditação) is the Portuguese national accreditation body, operating under Regulation (EC) 765/2008 and DL 23/2011. It is the only body in Portugal authorised to grant accreditation to inspection bodies under ISO/IEC 17020:2026.

The process is governed by:
- **DRC001** — General Regulation for Accreditation
- **DRC007** — Procedure for Accreditation of Inspection Bodies
- **OGC006/ILAC P15** — Specific Criteria for Inspection Bodies

Application forms: **DIC002** (general application) and **DIC004** (inspection body-specific technical details).

### The five phases

**Phase 1 — Preparation (Months 1–3)**
Gap analysis against ISO/IEC 17020:2026; legal entity formation; management system architecture design; documentation framework.

**Phase 2 — Documentation (Months 4–6)**
Quality manual; inspection procedures for each building system; competency framework for all inspector levels; impartiality risk assessment; management system documentation aligned to Option A.

**Phase 3 — Implementation (Months 7–10)**
Inspector recruitment and training (minimum 50 supervised inspections per inspector across diverse building types); pilot inspections; internal audit; management review.

**Phase 4 — IPAC Assessment (Months 11–14)**
Application submission via DIC002 + DIC004; document review by IPAC assessors; on-site assessment of the body's premises and processes; witness audits — IPAC assessors observe live inspections and assess inspector adherence to documented procedures. This is the critical gate: the inspector must demonstrate that they are actually doing what the procedures say, not just that the procedures exist.

**Phase 5 — Accreditation (Month 15+)**
Non-conformity closure; accreditation decision; certificate issuance. The accreditation specifies the exact scope (building types, inspection methods). Surveillance is annual; the full accreditation cycle is four years.

## 9. What this means for the Property Condition Score

The PCS derives its institutional credibility entirely from the PCA that feeds it. A score derived from an IPAC-accredited inspection has a fundamentally different standing than a score derived from an informal walk-through:

- **The input data has a documented audit trail.** Every observation, measurement, and photograph is traceable to a specific inspector, instrument, calibration record, and date.
- **The methodology is publicly documented.** Any bank, insurer, or regulator can examine the inspection procedure and verify that the score was produced consistently.
- **The body's independence is verified.** IPAC has confirmed that no commercial conflict of interest compromised the inspection — which is exactly what a mortgage underwriter or insurance actuary needs before they can price on the data.
- **The results are internationally recognised.** Via ILAC MRA, a PCS score derived from an IPAC-accredited inspection carries the same weight in Germany, France, the UK, or the Netherlands as in Portugal.

This is what transforms the PCS from a proprietary scoring product into institutional infrastructure. A bank cannot integrate an unverified score into its credit policy — the risk and compliance functions will not allow it. A bank can integrate a score derived from IPAC-accredited inspections into its underwriting workflow, because the accreditation provides exactly the due diligence trail the compliance function requires.

The accreditation does not just validate the inspection. It validates the entire data chain that makes the PCS commercially and institutionally viable.
